## How much does mold inspection testing cost in CA?

A standard residential mold inspection with lab-analyzed samples typically runs $300 to $650 in California, and can reach $700 to $1,200 for larger properties or more complex sampling. The final number depends mostly on how many samples the inspector collects and how hard the property is to access.

Here's how that typically breaks down by scope:

| Scenario | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Visual inspection only (no lab samples) | $150 - $300 |
| Standard inspection + 2-3 air samples | $300 - $550 |
| Full inspection + air and surface samples (4-6 samples) | $500 - $900 |
| Large home or multi-story property with crawl space/attic access | $700 - $1,200 |
| Post-flood or post-leak assessment with moisture mapping | $600 - $1,100 |
| Mold clearance testing after remediation | $300 - $600 |

These are typical industry ranges across California, not a quote. Every property is different, and a reputable inspector should walk you through the proposed scope and sample count before anything is collected.

## What factors affect the cost of a mold inspection?

The biggest cost drivers are the number of lab samples, the size and accessibility of the property, and whether the job needs specialty instruments like thermal imaging. A single-story home with one suspect area costs far less to test than a two-story property with crawl space and attic access.

Specific factors that move the price:

- **Number of samples.** Each air sample (collected with a calibrated pump pulling air through a spore trap cassette) and each surface sample (a tape lift or swab from a suspect area) adds lab analysis cost. Most homes need at least one outdoor baseline sample for comparison.
- **Property size and layout.** A 1970s single-story ranch common in Kingsburg and Selma is quicker to inspect than a multi-story Fresno home with multiple bathrooms and a finished basement-equivalent crawl space.
- **Accessibility.** Crawl spaces, attics, and areas behind built-in cabinetry take longer to inspect and often require more sampling.
- **Lab turnaround.** Standard lab analysis typically runs 24 to 72 hours; rush turnaround for time-sensitive real estate transactions usually costs more.
- **Specialty instruments.** Thermal imaging and moisture mapping add cost but can pinpoint hidden intrusion behind drywall without opening walls.
- **Scope: visual-only vs. lab-confirmed.** A visual inspection alone is cheaper but can't tell you what species or spore count is present, which is often what a buyer, lender, or health concern actually requires. The [EPA's guidance on mold testing and sampling](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-testing-or-sampling) notes that sampling isn't always necessary if visible growth is already confirmed, but it becomes important when results need to be documented or compared to an outdoor baseline.

## Who pays for mold testing, buyer or seller?

In most California real estate transactions, the buyer pays for mold testing as part of their inspection contingency, though it's negotiable and sometimes split or covered by the seller as a condition of closing. Some sellers order a pre-listing test to disclose findings upfront and avoid a last-minute renegotiation.

When a home inspector's general report flags "possible microbial growth" or moisture staining, that's usually a prompt for a specialized mold inspection, not a diagnosis. An independent, lab-backed report gives both sides a documented number to negotiate from instead of a guess. Because an independent tester doesn't perform remediation, the report has no incentive baked in either direction, which is exactly why lenders and real estate attorneys tend to trust it. ## What happens during a mold inspection?

A mold inspection moves through four phases: a visual walkthrough, targeted sampling, lab analysis, and a written report comparing results to an outdoor baseline. The whole process, from arrival to sample collection, usually takes one to two hours on-site.

1. **Visual inspection.** The inspector checks moisture-prone areas first, bathrooms, under-sink cabinets, attic sheathing, and crawl spaces, common trouble spots in older Central Valley homes with aging swamp coolers or slab foundations. They log visible staining and take moisture meter readings at suspect spots.
2. **Sampling.** Air samples are pulled through a spore trap cassette with a calibrated pump, both indoors at suspect areas and outdoors for a baseline comparison. Surface samples use a tape lift or swab wherever visible growth is suspected.
3. **Chain of custody.** Every sample is sealed, labeled, and logged with chain-of-custody paperwork before it's sent to an accredited third-party laboratory for spore count and species analysis.
4. **Report and next steps.** The lab results come back compared against the outdoor baseline, and the inspector delivers a written report. If levels are elevated, the next step is hiring a qualified, independent remediation contractor to do the physical work, then scheduling clearance testing to verify it actually worked.
