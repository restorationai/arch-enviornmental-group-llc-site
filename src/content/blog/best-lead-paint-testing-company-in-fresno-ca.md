**TL;DR:** The best lead paint testing company in Fresno is certified through the California Department of Public Health as a Lead Inspector/Assessor, uses an XRF analyzer calibrated to EPA protocol, provides a written report with room-by-room readings, and has no financial stake in whether lead is found, meaning they do not also perform the abatement. Confirm certification, ask about testing method, and read a sample report before you book.

If you're staring down a renovation permit on a Tower District bungalow, a daycare licensing requirement, or a real estate contingency on a Fig Garden home built before 1978, you don't have time to guess which lead testing company to trust. Most of Fresno's older housing stock predates the 1978 federal ban on residential lead paint, which means the question isn't whether a company CAN test for lead, it's whether the one you pick does it the right way, with the right credentials, and without a conflict of interest. Here's the checklist to work through before you call anyone.

## What should I look for in a lead paint testing company in Fresno?

Look for three things: state certification you can verify, a clear testing method, and a company that only tests, never abates. Those three criteria eliminate most of the risk in hiring the wrong firm.

A Lead Inspector/Assessor certification is issued through California's Childhood Lead Poisoning Prevention Branch, and it's checkable against the state's professional directory before you ever let anyone into your home. Ask for the inspector's certificate number directly. A legitimate inspector will give it to you without hesitation, because it's a matter of public record.

The testing method matters almost as much. Fresno has a mix of 1940s-1970s construction, from downtown bungalows to postwar tract homes in Clovis and Sanger, and the right sampling approach depends on the surfaces involved. A company that can explain its method in plain terms, not just "we test for lead," is one that actually understands the work.

## XRF testing vs lab sampling: which one do I need?

XRF (X-ray fluorescence) testing gives instant, non-destructive readings on painted surfaces and is the standard method for most residential inspections; lab-based sampling (paint chips sent for PLM or ICP analysis) is used when a surface can't be read directly or when a lab confirmation is required for a legal or regulatory record.

An XRF analyzer is held directly against a painted surface, a window casing, a baseboard, a door jamb, and gives a reading in seconds, measured in milligrams per square centimeter. It doesn't damage the surface, which matters on original trim in older homes. Lab sampling involves removing a small paint chip and sending it to an accredited laboratory, useful for porous or oddly shaped surfaces an XRF can't read cleanly, or when a client needs documented lab chain-of-custody for a legal or insurance matter.

Either method is legitimate. What matters is that the company you hire can explain which one applies to your property and why, rather than defaulting to whichever is cheapest for them to run.

## What should a lead inspection report actually include?

A complete lead inspection report lists every surface tested, the reading for each one, and a clear determination of lead-based paint presence per federal and state thresholds, organized by room so a contractor or buyer can act on it directly.

At minimum, expect: a floor plan or room-by-room breakdown, the XRF or lab reading for each tested component (window, door, trim, wall, exterior siding), the inspector's certification number, and a plain-language summary of what was found and where. If you're using the report for a real estate transaction or a [EPA Lead Renovation, Repair and Painting Program](https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-renovation-repair-and-painting-program) compliance decision before a remodel, the report needs to stand on its own, readable by a contractor, a lender, or an attorney without a follow-up call to decode it. Before you sign a contract, ask to see a sample report with identifying details redacted.

If your property also has textured ceilings, floor tile, or siding from a similar era, it's worth asking whether the same inspection visit should include an [asbestos testing](/services/asbestos-testing/) check, since both materials were common in the same construction period and a combined visit often makes more sense than two separate appointments.

## Should the lead testing company also do the removal work?

No. The strongest lead testing companies test only and refer remediation to a separate, qualified contractor, because a company that profits from finding lead has an incentive to find it.

This is the single most important question to ask before you book. If a company quotes you a testing fee and then tells you abatement work with the same crew is the fast path forward, that's a conflict of interest worth walking away from. An independent tester has no stake in the outcome of your results. Their report should work with any licensed abatement contractor you choose, and if your results come back positive, the honest next step is: hire a qualified, unaffiliated abatement contractor, then bring in an independent tester again for clearance testing once the work is done. Clearance testing verifies the contractor's work, which is exactly why it shouldn't come from the contractor.

## What should I do before I book an inspection?

Before booking, ask for the inspector's certification number, confirm the testing method for your property type, and request a sample report, then compare answers across two or three companies before you decide.

That's a 15-minute phone call, not a research project.
