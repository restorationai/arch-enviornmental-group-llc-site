## When do you actually need a certified inspector instead of a DIY kit?

Call a certified lead inspector any time the result has to hold up for a renovation permit, a real estate transaction, or a tenant dispute. A hardware-store swab test only gives a rough yes-or-no on one spot and isn't accepted as a determination method under [the EPA's Lead Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) rule](https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-renovation-repair-and-painting-program).

Sodium sulfide swab kits react with lead compounds, but they can miss lead hidden under newer top coats, and they don't quantify how much lead is in the paint or where it sits in the layer history. For a pre-1978 Kingsburg home (federal law banned residential lead paint that year, per [the EPA](https://www.epa.gov/lead)), that distinction matters: a contractor can't legally treat the area as lead-free based on a drugstore kit, and an insurer or buyer's agent generally won't either.

A certified inspector uses an XRF (X-ray fluorescence) analyzer that reads the lead content through the paint layers in seconds, non-destructively, component by component: window casings, baseboards, door jambs, exterior trim. That's the version of "lead paint testing" that actually satisfies a permit office, a disclosure form, or an RRP-certified renovator's paperwork.

## Lead inspection or lead risk assessment, which one do you need?

A lead inspection tells you whether lead-based paint is present and exactly where; a lead risk assessment goes further and identifies which of those surfaces are an actual hazard right now, plus what it would take to control them. Pick the inspection when you're clearing a renovation scope or a sale; pick the risk assessment when there's deteriorated paint, lead dust, or a documented health concern already on the table.

Most pre-renovation and pre-sale calls only need the inspection: a component-by-component survey that flags which surfaces contain lead paint so the contractor can plan around them. A risk assessment adds dust wipe sampling and a condition survey of peeling, chipping, or friction surfaces (windows, doors) and ends with a hazard-reduction recommendation, not just a yes or no. If a child in the household has had an elevated blood lead test, or a landlord has an active complaint, the risk assessment is the right scope, not the basic inspection.

## What happens when you call, and how do XRF readings and lab samples differ?

Most of the job is handled with the XRF reader on-site; paint chip samples only go to an accredited lab when a surface is too curved, textured, or substrate-dense for a reliable direct reading. XRF gives you same-visit numbers for most components; chip samples add a few business days for destructive lab analysis.

When you call, have the property's approximate build year, the rooms or exterior elements in scope, and your timeline (permit deadline, closing date, move-in date) ready. On-site, the inspector scans painted components in a defined pattern, documents readings against each substrate, and pulls a small paint chip sample into a labeled, chain-of-custody bag only where the XRF reading is inconclusive. You get a written report listing every tested component, its result, and whether it's classified as lead-based paint under the applicable threshold.

To confirm any inspector's credentials before you book (ours or anyone's), the California Department of Public Health maintains the state registry of certified lead professionals. Ask for the certification number directly and check it against that registry; a legitimate inspector will hand it over without hesitation.

A positive lead paint result means you hire a qualified, RRP-certified or lead-abatement contractor to do the actual work, not the company that tested it.
