## What does a mold clearance test actually include?

A mold clearance test, also called post-remediation verification, includes a visual inspection of the finished work area, indoor air samples compared against an outdoor baseline, and laboratory analysis of what's collected. The inspector checks that visible mold, staining, and debris are gone and that containment was removed cleanly, then pulls air through spore trap cassettes at multiple points inside the remediated space.

Those indoor readings only mean something next to an outdoor sample taken the same day. Mold spores exist everywhere outside; the test is asking whether indoor spore types and counts have returned to something close to that outdoor baseline, not whether the air is spore-free. The cassettes go to an accredited laboratory, and the report comes back as a pass or fail against that comparison, following the kind of protocol outlined in [EPA's guide to mold remediation in buildings](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-remediation-schools-and-commercial-buildings-guide-chapter-3). Surface swabs or tape lifts get added when a wall, duct, or piece of furniture is still in question.

## Why shouldn't the company that removed your mold also clear it?

The remediation contractor should never be the one who signs off on their own work, because they have a direct financial interest in a passing result. A company that is paid to remove mold and then paid again to confirm it's gone has no incentive to flag a job that needs to be redone.

An independent testing company has the opposite incentive structure: findings do not change what the testing company gets paid, so there is no reason to call a space clear when it isn't, and no reason to call it contaminated when it's fine. ## How soon after remediation should you schedule clearance testing, and what if it fails?

Schedule clearance testing as soon as the remediation contractor reports the containment is down and the area is dry, typically within a day or two of their final walkthrough, before anyone moves furniture back in or closes up drywall. Testing too early, while containment is still sealed or surfaces are still damp, risks a false reading that doesn't reflect the finished space.

If a clearance test fails, it means indoor spore counts or types are still elevated compared to the outdoor baseline, or visible growth remains. That's not a dead end: it tells the remediation contractor exactly where to go back in, whether that's a missed section of subfloor, a duct that wasn't cleaned, or moisture that hasn't fully dried. You then schedule a retest once the additional work is done. A failed first pass is common enough that a serious clearance company builds the possibility of a retest into how they explain the process up front, rather than treating it as a surprise.

## What do you do with the clearance letter?

A clearance letter documents that a remediated area passed independent testing, and it's the document insurers, buyers, and property managers ask for before releasing funds or closing a transaction. Home sales in escrow, insurance claim payouts tied to a covered water loss, and rental turnovers after a mold complaint all commonly require one.

The letter is only as credible as the company behind it, which is why lenders, agents, and adjusters increasingly ask specifically whether the tester was affiliated with the remediation crew. A report from an independent, accredited source avoids that question entirely.
