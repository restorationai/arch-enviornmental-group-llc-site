In Kingsburg, CA, mold clearance testing (also called post-remediation verification) typically costs **$350 to $900**, depending on how many rooms are tested, whether an outdoor baseline air sample is included, and how many lab samples the inspector collects. A single contained room usually lands at the low end; a whole-house clearance with indoor and outdoor comparison samples runs higher.

If you just had mold removed from a bathroom ceiling, a crawl space, or an entire HVAC system after a leak, you're probably staring down a contractor's invoice marked "work complete" and wondering who actually verifies that. Clearance testing is that verification step, and it only means something when the person doing it had nothing to do with the removal.

## What does mold clearance testing actually cost?

Most Kingsburg-area clearance jobs fall between $350 and $900, scaled by scope and sample count. Here's a typical breakdown by scenario:

| Scenario | Typical cost range |
|---|---|
| Single-room post-remediation check (1-2 air samples) | $350 - $450 |
| Whole-house clearance, 3-5 rooms, indoor + outdoor baseline | $500 - $750 |
| Clearance with added tape-lift or surface sampling | $450 - $650 |
| Crawl space or attic clearance after remediation | $350 - $550 |
| Commercial or multi-unit clearance, per unit | $600 - $1,200 |
| Rush lab turnaround add-on | $75 - $150 |

These are industry-typical ranges, not a quote. Every property is different, and a written scope should always be confirmed before the inspector arrives.

## What drives the price up or down?

The number of rooms, the sample count, and the turnaround speed are what move the number most. A one-room bathroom clearance with a single indoor air sample and no outdoor baseline is the cheapest version of this test. Add rooms, add an outdoor comparison sample (standard practice so the lab has a true indoor-to-outdoor spore count comparison), or add surface sampling like tape lifts on a suspect wall, and the price climbs.

Property size matters too. A 1960s Kingsburg farmhouse near the orchard edge of town with a full crawl space has more square footage to verify than a single remodeled bathroom in a newer stucco ranch. Lab turnaround tier also factors in: standard analysis typically runs on a multi-day cycle, while rush processing for a closing deadline or insurance claim adds a flat fee.

## Who pays for mold clearance testing?

It depends on why you're testing. If remediation followed an insurance claim (a covered water loss, for example), the clearance test is often part of the claim's closeout documentation, and some policies will reimburse it as part of the covered work. Check your policy language; coverage for mold-related testing is frequently limited or capped even on a covered loss, per the [EPA's guidance on mold remediation](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-remediation-schools-and-commercial-buildings-guide-chapter-3).

In a real estate transaction, clearance testing after a disclosed mold issue is usually negotiated between buyer and seller, similar to any other repair-verification cost. Landlords clearing a unit for re-occupancy typically absorb it as part of the remediation project, since a documented clearance letter protects them if a tenant later raises a complaint.

## What happens during a clearance test?

1. **Timing**: testing happens after the remediation contractor has finished removal, cleaning, and containment teardown, not immediately after the last spore is wiped. A short settling period lets any disturbed dust resettle before air samples are drawn.
2. **Visual check**: the inspector walks the remediated area looking for visible residue, residual moisture, or signs the work wasn't fully completed.
3. **Indoor air sampling**: a calibrated pump draws air through a spore trap cassette in each previously affected room.
4. **Outdoor baseline sample**: a comparison sample is pulled from outside the structure, since indoor spore counts are judged against outdoor ambient levels, not an absolute number.
5. **Lab analysis**: cassettes go to an accredited third-party laboratory under chain of custody. The report comes back with spore counts and species, compared indoor to outdoor.
6. **Clearance letter**: a written report states whether indoor levels compare favorably to outdoor baseline, which is what insurers, buyers, and property managers actually want to see.

## What happens if the clearance test fails?

A failed clearance means indoor spore counts or species still read elevated compared to the outdoor baseline, and it means the remediation contractor needs to go back in. This isn't rare: remediation sometimes misses a hidden pocket of growth behind a wall cavity or under flooring that wasn't part of the original scope. The written report documents exactly where the elevated counts were found, which gives the contractor a clear target instead of guessing.

This is also where independence matters most. A tester with no stake in the remediation contract has no reason to pass work that isn't done, and no reason to fail work that is.
