## Who do you call first: a tester, a plumber, or the water provider?

It depends on whether you already know what's wrong or you're trying to find out. If you have a taste, odor, staining, or health concern and no confirmed cause, call an independent tester first. If you already know the plumbing itself is broken, call a plumber or well contractor instead.

Kingsburg sits over the same Central Valley aquifer that feeds the orchards and vineyards surrounding the city, and a meaningful share of households on the outskirts, outside the municipal service area anchored near downtown's Swedish Village and Memorial Park, draw from private wells rather than city water. Private wells have no utility testing them for you; that responsibility sits entirely with the homeowner. If you're buying a property with a private well near the Kings River corridor or anywhere outside city limits, an independent water test is standard due diligence, the same way a mold or radon test is.

Call a plumber when the issue is clearly mechanical: a visible leak, sudden pressure drop, or discoloration you can trace to a specific corroded pipe or fitting. Call a well driller or pump contractor when the well itself needs mechanical work, a new pump, deepening, or a casing repair. Call the City of Kingsburg's public works line if you're on municipal water and want the system-wide Consumer Confidence Report rather than a test of your own tap. None of those calls tell you what's actually in your water today; only a lab test does that, and that's the part an independent tester handles.

## What happens when you call?

When you call, Arch asks a handful of quick questions: whether the source is a private well or municipal connection, what symptom prompted the call (odor, taste, color, a health concern, or a pending real estate transaction), and whether there's any known history of nearby agricultural runoff, a failed septic system, or recent plumbing work. Have your address and, if it's a well, any known well depth or construction details on hand; it speeds up scheduling and tells the inspector which contaminant panel actually makes sense for your property.

On arrival, the inspector collects water samples directly from the tap or wellhead following a documented chain-of-custody process, the paper trail that proves the sample wasn't tampered with between your property and the lab bench. Samples go to an accredited third-party laboratory, never an in-house lab, which is part of why the result holds up in a transaction or a dispute. Typical panels for Central Valley properties screen for total coliform bacteria, nitrate (a common concern near agricultural land, per the [EPA's drinking water standards](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water)), and naturally occurring inorganics like arsenic, which shows up in groundwater across parts of the San Joaquin Valley. The [CDC's guidance on private well water](https://www.cdc.gov/healthywater/drinking/private/wells/index.html) is a useful plain-language reference if you want to understand why these specific contaminants get tested before you ever pick up the phone.

## What if the results show a problem?

If a contaminant comes back above a recommended level, the next step is hiring a licensed, unaffiliated well or water treatment contractor to install the right fix, whether that's a filtration system, a disinfection step, or well repair. Because Arch doesn't sell or install treatment equipment, there's no incentive baked into the report to find a problem that isn't there, and the findings work with whatever licensed contractor you choose, not a preferred vendor. Once that contractor finishes the work, the right move is independent clearance testing, a fresh sample pulled and analyzed the same way, to confirm the fix actually worked. A contractor verifying their own installation isn't the same as a neutral third party confirming it.

If you're mid-transaction and the clock is tight, get the independent test scheduled first; a documented, lab-backed result gives you and the other party something concrete to negotiate around, rather than a guess based on taste or smell.
